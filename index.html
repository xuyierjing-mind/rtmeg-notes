00 · Start here

A beginner’s map of how data leave the MEGIN scanner and reach your MATLAB code fast enough to act on.

“Real-time” here means: while the subject is still in the scanner, a program reads the newest few hundred milliseconds of MEG data, computes something (a power value, a frequency, a phase), and possibly triggers something (a stimulus). The whole site follows that one chain:

MEG sensors + DSPs28-sample packets
Acquisition PCGo / Stop / Record
neuromag2ftchunks of N samples
FieldTrip bufferTCP, port 1972
MATLABpoll, read, compute
Stimulus / outputPTB + trigger

01 · Principle: the buffer

Why you do not read the amplifier directly.

The problem

MATLAB runs one thing at a time. If it spends 200 ms computing an FFT, new samples arriving during those 200 ms could be lost. The operating system keeps network data for a while, but nobody guarantees for how long [S1].

The solution: a waiting room

The FieldTrip buffer is a small server that receives data from the acquisition side in small blocks and keeps them while your program is busy. Your MATLAB code later asks, “anything new?” and picks up what it missed [S1]. Think of a conveyor belt with a bounded length.

Writeracquisition software
Bufferheader · samples · events
Reader 1MATLAB: power
Reader 2viewer

Three kinds of content

ItemWhat it isMATLAB call
HeaderSampling rate, channel count and names, number of samples so farft_read_header
SamplesThe signal itself, channels × timeft_read_data
EventsTriggers or markers with a sample numberft_read_event

Useful properties

  • Many programs can read at once, even on different computers [S1].
  • The buffer is a ring: when it fills, old data are overwritten, so you cannot always read back to sample 1 [S2].
  • Clients exist for MATLAB, C/C++, Java and Python. Python is client-only [S3], which matters for the “Python later” plan.
  • The writer can flush the buffer, which is what happens when a new run starts [S1].

Is it fast enough?

The buffer’s own speed is rarely your bottleneck. FieldTrip’s benchmark reports it moving hundreds of thousands of samples per second on old hardware, and local connections are faster and steadier than remote ones [S4]. The delay you will notice comes from block size and acquisition, covered in 03.

Detail you can skip: the wire protocol (buffer_protocol) and the C reference implementation matter only if you write your own server or client [S3].

02 · Start streaming

The routine at the MEGIN acquisition computer, based on the operator manual.

A. Try it without the scanner (recommended first)

FieldTrip ships small programs: start buffer, then sine2ft (a fake amplifier), then viewer. Click start in sine2ft, connect in the viewer, and you see a sine wave streaming. Then, in MATLAB [S2]:

hdr = ft_read_header('buffer://localhost:1972')   % run repeatedly
hdr.nSamples   % should keep growing

B. At the scanner: per-run routine (rtMEG manual)

  1. Open a terminal in /home/neuromag/fieldtrip-20121231/realtime/src/acquisition/neuromag/bin/x86_64pc-linux-gnu (right-click → Open Terminal Here) and wait for the experimenter.
  2. When the run is about to begin, type ./neuromag2ft --chunksize 30 and press Enter (the up-arrow recalls the previous command). 30 samples at 1000 Hz = 30 ms per update. Do not go below 28.
  3. Experimenter presses Go, then Record raw. Data now flow into the buffer.
  4. At the end the experimenter presses Stop and saves. Data stop updating, but can still be read until rtMEG is stopped.
  5. After confirming with the subject, press Ctrl-C in the terminal and check that the prompt returns.
  6. Next run: back to step 2. When everything is done, close the terminal.
Why Ctrl-C matters. neuromag2ft temporarily changes the acquisition computer’s buffer length and restores it on exit. If it crashes, --fixchunksize resets it [S5].
A discrepancy to resolve. The AI notes advise 5–10 ms chunks to cut jitter (unverified). The rtMEG documentation says the DSPs deliver 28 samples per packet and that values under 28 are unwise; the authors ran 29 with a delay under 50 ms [S5], and your manual also forbids going below 28. So at 1000 Hz, ≈28–30 ms is the practical floor. Plan around that.

Tips from the sources

  • Smaller chunk = less delay but more load on the acquisition computer. Closing the Acquisition visualizer window reportedly helps with small chunks [S5].
  • Your manual recommends restarting rtMEG every run, and recalls streaming failing after about 10 minutes (a recollection, not a tested limit).
  • Reading from another computer: give the reader the acquisition computer’s name and port (e.g. buffer://sinuhe:1972); firewalls must allow the port [S5].

C. Internal trigger generator (for timing tests)

The scanner can produce its own pulses, which is handy for the delay tests in 03.

  1. In Acquisition: Change next to “External stimulus generation”.
  2. Choose Internal stimulus generation.
  3. Default settings: # = 1, length = 5 ms, ISI = 500 ms, then Ok.
  4. After Go, click Stimulate to start the pulses; click again to stop.

03 · Delay & jitter

Two different problems: a constant lag you can subtract, and a wobble you cannot.

  • Delay (latency): how late on average. Measure it once, then compensate.
  • Jitter: how much it varies trial to trial. This sets your precision.

Where time is lost

StageTypical sizeFixed or variable
DSP packets (28 samples)≈28 ms at 1 kHz [S5]variable
neuromag2ft chunk= chunksize in msvariable (0 to chunk)
rtMEG end to endunder 50 ms at chunk 29 [S5]both
Your polling loop1–a few ms; ft_read_header took ≈5.7 ms per call on old hardware [S6]variable
Your computationmeasure it with tic/tocmostly fixed
Waiting for the next screen refresh0 to 16.7 ms at 60 Hzvariable
Projector to light≈9–10 ms measured at one MEG lab with PROPixx [S9]mostly fixed
Continuous head localisation (cHPI) made data arrive in bursts at the CTF lab, about 100 ms [S6]. That is a different vendor, but with cHPI on it is worth testing separately on your system (not verified for MEGIN).

Interactive: what does the wobble cost in phase?

Rough model: each variable stage adds a uniform 0–X ms; after subtracting the average, the leftover is about ± half the total spread. Real stages are not exactly uniform or independent. Use it for intuition, then measure.

How to measure: the trigger-echo loop

FieldTrip’s method [S6]: send a pulse into a trigger channel, detect it online through the buffer, immediately write a second pulse (different value) to the same channel, record both, and compute the time difference offline.

trigger → acquisition → buffer → MATLAB detects → echo trigger → back into the recording
delay = (sample_echo − sample_trigger) / Fs

Check also that no triggers are missed, and plot echo sample against trigger sample to see whether delay accumulates over time [S6]. Their pulses were sent over a serial port to a parallel-port trigger input; on your system the equivalent is whichever trigger inputs you have, so adapt the wiring and channel (the AI notes name STI101, unverified).

Measuring the screen

  • Put a small white square in a corner of the stimulus and tape a photodiode over it, wired to an analog channel. It records when light really appeared. A photodiode adds only ≈0.5 ms bias in PTB’s own tests [S8].
  • Psychtoolbox timestamps Screen('Flip') at the real buffer swap, and its documentation reports jitter under 100 µs on Linux under normal conditions [S7].
  • Setting Priority to real-time scheduling helps under system load [S8].
Rule of thumb: compensate the constant lag, then shrink the jitter, then verify with a photodiode. If the final scatter is bigger than your design tolerates, accept it and sort trials by the measured phase afterwards.

04 · MATLAB scripts

From “see the data” to “compute something every chunk”.

Always test with a fake source before connecting to the scanner. Start MATLAB with FieldTrip on the path: addpath('your/fieldtrip'); ft_defaults.

1 · Signal viewer (FieldTrip example [S10])

Two MATLAB sessions; the first writes random data into the buffer, the second displays it, updating every second.

% session 1: fake amplifier
cfg = []; cfg.channel = 1:10; cfg.blocksize = 1; cfg.fsample = 250;
cfg.target.dataset = 'buffer://localhost:1972';
ft_realtime_signalproxy(cfg)

% session 2: viewer
cfg = []; cfg.blocksize = 1; cfg.dataset = 'buffer://localhost:1972';
ft_realtime_signalviewer(cfg)

At the scanner, point cfg.dataset at the acquisition computer instead, e.g. 'buffer://sinuhe:1972'.

2 · Online power spectrum [S11]

cfg = []; cfg.blocksize = 1; cfg.foilim = [0 30];
cfg.dataset = 'buffer://localhost:1972';
ft_realtime_powerestimate(cfg)

Reads small chunks, estimates the spectrum per chunk, shows the average over chosen channels. A good first look at alpha.

3 · A minimal polling loop you can build on

My simplified sketch (untested; the idea follows the FieldTrip loops [S2, S6]):

buf = 'buffer://localhost:1972';
hdr = ft_read_header(buf, 'cache', true);
fs  = hdr.Fs;  win = round(0.5*fs);          % 500 ms window
idx = match_str(hdr.label, ft_channelselection('MEGGRAD', hdr.label));
prev = hdr.nSamples;  chunk = 30;
while true
  hdr = ft_read_header(buf, 'cache', true);
  if hdr.nSamples >= prev + chunk && hdr.nSamples >= win
    e = hdr.nSamples;  b = e - win + 1;
    dat = ft_read_data(buf, 'header', hdr, 'begsample', b, ...
                       'endsample', e, 'chanindx', idx);
    x = mean(dat, 1);  x = x - mean(x);       % crude single trace
    alpha = bandpower(x, fs, [8 12]);        % Signal Processing Toolbox
    prev = e;  fprintf('alpha power %.3g\n', alpha);
  end
  pause(0.001);                              % keep ~1 kHz polling
end
  • FieldTrip’s later examples replace the header read with ft_poll_buffer to learn how many new samples exist [S6]. Try it if the header read feels slow.
  • Keep the loop short. If computing takes longer than the chunk interval, you fall behind; log toc per iteration to see.
  • Averaging all gradiometers is only a placeholder. Better: a fixed weight vector from a baseline recording (05).

4 · Jitter-aware stimulus timing (Psychtoolbox idea)

Do not wait until the condition is true and then flip, because you will always lose up to one frame. Instead, ask what the state will be at the next flip. From the AI notes (unverified in detail, standard PTB practice for the scheduling call [S7, S8]):

% lookahead = (time to next refresh) + projector lag (from photodiode)
pred = est_phase + 2*pi*f*lookahead;       % predicted phase at onset
if abs(angle(exp(1i*(pred - target))))  < tol && amp > thr
    Screen('DrawTexture', win, tex);
    vbl = Screen('Flip', win, vblNext - 0.5*ifi);   % schedule the flip
end

At 60 Hz this keeps the display error within about half a frame (±8 ms) rather than a full frame, which the notes translate to roughly ±30° at 10 Hz (consistent with 360 × 10 × 0.0083).

Always keep a photodiode log. Outside timing studies report real onset can differ from the software timestamp, so re-bin trials by measured onset [S7, S9].

05 · What to compute online

Power, frequency and phase differ a lot in difficulty.

QuantityDifficultyDelay sensitivityBeginner path
Power (e.g. alpha)EasyLow: a 100–500 ms window is fineft_realtime_powerestimate, or bandpower
Peak frequencyEasy–mediumLowSpectrum peak in a longer window
PhaseHardHigh: 10 ms ≈ 36° at 10 HzSee methods below

Phase shift = 360 × frequency × delay. So 10 Hz × 10 ms = 36°.

Suggestion: build power monitoring first. It exercises the whole chain (buffer, polling, channel selection, timing log) without phase accuracy risk. Add phase later.

Phase estimation without seeing the future

A standard Hilbert transform needs data on both sides of the moment you care about; online you only have the past. Your notes list four families (unverified; check the original papers):

MethodIdeaWatch out
AR forecast + Hilbert (e.g. phastimate, Zrenner et al. 2018)Filter, extrapolate the wave forward, then HilbertNeeds good SNR; tune window and model order per person
State-space / Kalman (SSPE, Wodeyar et al. 2021)Track a rotating vector; gives uncertaintyFit parameters on baseline data
ecHT (Schreglmann et al. 2021)Corrects Hilbert end distortion in frequency domainResolution limited by window
Zero-crossing / peak timingPredict next peak from past cycle lengthOnly for slow, regular rhythms

Three design habits (from the notes, widely used)

  • Compensate the fixed delay: shift the target phase by 360 × f × delay.
  • Gate by amplitude: phase is meaningless when the rhythm is weak; trigger only above a threshold.
  • Reduce 306 channels to one trace: compute a weight vector offline from a 2–3 minute baseline (e.g. beamformer or ICA), then one matrix product per chunk. The notes describe this plus a noise-projection step (unverified details).

Your Alpha/Theta, visual-detection design is discussed at length in the notes; I deliberately left the experiment-specific parts (QUEST, Gabor rendering) out of this beginner site.

08 · Contribute & change log

How the lab keeps these notes correct.

How to contribute

  1. Open the repository (github.com/xuyierjing-mind/rtmeg-notes) and create a branch.
  2. Edit index.html. Each page is one <section id="pN">; keep the order of pages 00–08.
  3. Cite every outside fact as [S#] and add the source to page 07. Mark anything not checked against the original as “unverified”.
  4. For lab measurements (page 03), write the date, system, software version, chunk size, and whether cHPI was on.
  5. Add one line to the change log below and to CHANGELOG.md.
  6. Open a pull request. One maintainer merges it, then reloads the published site to check it.
Keep it beginner-level. If a detail is only needed to write your own server or protocol, link to the FieldTrip page instead of copying it.
